Original work 2023 · reproducible edition 2026

Abstract

This study examines 569,127 administrative crime records in Colombia, victimisation patterns in Bogotá, and the municipal distribution of illicit crops in 2015. Municipal aggregation, Pearson correlation, contingency tables and exploratory visualisation in R reveal territorial associations, gender contrasts and spatial concentration.

1. Research questions

The study evaluates the association between municipal theft and homicide counts, crime concentration by category, victimisation by sex and weapon in Bogotá, and the municipal distribution of illicit crops.

2. Data and methods

The public-safety module contains 569,127 records and 20 variables. Theft combines offences against persons and residences. Counts by category, the share of female victims and composition tables are calculated. The rural module summarises agricultural production and land areas; for 2015 it estimates illicit-crop area as a share of total cultivated area.

Responsible interpretation. Administrative counts are not rates. Reporting behaviour, institutional coverage and population size may explain municipal differences.

3. Results

3.1 Theft and homicide

The report finds a correlation of r = 0.8386: municipalities with more recorded thefts also tend to have more homicides. Both counts rise with population size, so this is not causal evidence.

3.2 Urban concentration

The largest counts occur primarily in Bogotá, Cali, Medellín and Barranquilla. Personal injuries, sexual offences, threats and theft from persons are among the highest-volume categories.

Crime interpretation and analysis

According to the charts, personal injuries are the most frequently reported offence. Sexual offences, threats and theft from persons also occur at high frequencies in Colombia.

Residential burglary is particularly prominent in Bogotá: its count is almost four times that of Cali, the next municipality affected by this problem.

For offences such as cattle theft, livestock-related crime and cargo piracy, small and rural municipalities appear more often. Kidnapping in Arauca also stands out: the departmental capital and three major municipalities appear in the top 10. The report argues that this pattern warrants focused attention from public authorities.

Overall, municipalities with the largest crime counts tend to be those with the largest populations. A more rigorous analysis should therefore incorporate municipal population and calculate, for example, offences per 100,000 inhabitants.

Critical note in the web edition. This interpretation concerns administrative counts. The apparent concentration may change when rates, consistent periods, under-reporting and institutional coverage are considered.

3.3 Victimisation in Bogotá

Weapon classification includes 40,123 “other”, 35,322 no-weapon events, 17,180 sharp-weapon events and 11,840 firearm events. Men account for 89% of recorded homicide victims; women account for 87% of sexual-offence victims and 83% of domestic-violence victims.

3.4 Rural structures

Puerto Asís, Piamonte, San Miguel, Puerto Caicedo and Valle del Guamuez lead the 2015 illicit-crop share ranking.

Territorial interpretation and analysis of illicit crops

Municipalities with the highest percentages are concentrated mainly in southern and western Colombia. The original report links this geographic pattern to climatic conditions favourable to plant varieties used in the production of illicit substances.

It also proposes socioeconomic factors: low educational attainment and limited access to formal education, high levels of informal employment and precarious labour conditions. Poverty is another relevant dimension, highlighting significant challenges for development and social well-being in these territories.

Finally, the report identifies the presence of illegal armed groups as part of this territorial complexity and as a possible factor associated with the persistence of illegal activities and illicit crops.

Critical note in the web edition. These relationships are contextual hypotheses from the original report. The available data do not estimate causal effects or statistically test climate, education, informality, poverty or armed-group presence.

4. Limitations

  • Records depend on reporting and institutional capacity.
  • Counts were not adjusted for population or temporal exposure.
  • The revised version corrects a missing homicide filter in the historical script.
  • The original microdata were not included with the supplied documents.
  • Territorial associations do not identify causal mechanisms.

5. Conclusions

Combining statistics and territory supports public-policy questions, but every visualisation requires denominators, traceability and interpretive boundaries. Future research should incorporate rates per 100,000 inhabitants, comparable periods and spatial models.

Reproducibility

The revised analysis is stored in R/analysis.R, the source manuscript in article.Rmd, and the historical script in archive/original_analysis.R.
